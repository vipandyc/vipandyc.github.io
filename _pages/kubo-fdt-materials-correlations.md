# From Fluctuations to Materials Properties: Kubo, FDT, and Correlations

An equilibrium material still has fluctuating velocities, currents, dipoles, and spins. We will derive how a weak applied field probes those fluctuations, then use the result to calculate transport coefficients and spectra.

The recurring choice is the observable: velocity for diffusion, stress for viscosity, currents for conductivity, and dipole or polarizability for optical spectra. Phonon and electron densities of states (DOS) are included as related spectra; they count modes or one-particle states rather than measure a transport response.

## 1. From a Weak Field to a Response Function

Let $H$ be the unperturbed Hamiltonian and $T$ the absolute temperature. Write $k_{\rm B}$ for Boltzmann's constant, $\hbar$ for the reduced Planck constant, $\beta=1/(k_{\rm B}T)$, and $\langle O\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H}O)/Z$ with $Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}$. An operator's time dependence, $A(t)=e^{iHt/\hbar}Ae^{-iHt/\hbar}$, is generated by $H$. We apply a small field $f(t)$ through $H'(t)=-f(t)B$: $B$ is the quantity coupled to the field, and $A$ is the quantity measured. Write $\delta\langle A\rangle=\langle A\rangle_f-\langle A\rangle_0$ for the change caused by the field; separately, $\delta A=A-\langle A\rangle_0$ denotes an equilibrium fluctuation.

To first order in $f$, expand the interaction-picture evolution operator, which describes evolution due to $H'$ after the evolution due to $H$ has been factored out: $U_I=1-(i/\hbar)\int_{-\infty}^t H'_I(s)\,\mathrm ds+O(f^2)$. Inserting it into $\langle U_I^\dagger A(t)U_I\rangle$ gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta\langle A(t)\rangle
&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{t}f(s)
  \langle[A(t),B(s)]\rangle\,\mathrm ds,\\
\chi_{AB}(t-s)
&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t-s)
  \langle[A(t),B(s)]\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here $[A,B]=AB-BA$, $\Theta(t)=1$ for $t>0$ and $0$ for $t<0$, and equilibrium time-translation symmetry makes the average depend only on $t-s$. Thus the **quantum Kubo formula** can be written

<div class="math-display">
$$
\delta\langle A(t)\rangle
=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\chi_{AB}(t-s)f(s)\,\mathrm ds,
\qquad
\chi_{AB}(t)
=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t)
\left\langle[A(t),B(0)]\right\rangle_{\rm eq}.
$$
</div>

The first subscript of $\chi_{AB}$ names the measured observable; the second names the field's conjugate observable. For example, $H'=-EM$ and $A=M$ give the dipole response $\chi_{MM}$. Causality is encoded by $\Theta(t)$. Throughout, $\omega$ is angular frequency and $F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}F(t)\,\mathrm dt$; the frequency-domain response is $\delta\langle A\rangle(\omega)=\chi_{AB}(\omega)f(\omega)$.

For a real sinusoidal field, $\operatorname{Re}\chi_{AA}(\omega)$ gives the part of the response in phase with the field and $\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega)$ gives the out-of-phase part. The latter produces absorbed power, as derived in the infrared example below.

For classical dynamics, positions and momenta replace operators. Write $\Gamma=(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N;\mathbf p_1,\ldots,\mathbf p_N)$ and $\mathrm d\Gamma=\prod_i\mathrm d^3r_i\,\mathrm d^3p_i$. The **Poisson bracket** (PB) and the unperturbed time derivative are

<div class="math-display">
$$
\{F,G\}_{\rm PB}
=\sum_{i,\alpha}\left(
\frac{\partial F}{\partial r_{i\alpha}}
\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_{i\alpha}}
-\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{i\alpha}}
\frac{\partial G}{\partial r_{i\alpha}}\right),
\qquad
\dot B=\frac{\mathrm d B}{\mathrm dt}=\{B,H\}_{\rm PB}.
$$
</div>

The sum runs over particles $i$ and Cartesian directions $\alpha$. Hamilton's equation under $H+H'$ is $\mathrm dA/\mathrm dt=\{A,H\}_{\rm PB}-f(t)\{A,B\}_{\rm PB}$. Integrating its field-dependent term to first order, with all observables evolved under $H$, gives the classical response

<div class="math-display">
$$
\delta\langle A(t)\rangle_{\rm cl}
=\int_{-\infty}^t\chi_{AB}^{\rm cl}(t-s)f(s)\,\mathrm ds,
\qquad
\chi_{AB}^{\rm cl}(t)
=-\Theta(t)\langle\{A(t),B(0)\}_{\rm PB}\rangle.
$$
</div>

The superscript “cl” means classical. Now set $\beta=1/(k_{\rm B}T)$ and $\rho_{\rm eq}(\Gamma)=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta H(\Gamma)}$, where $Z=\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,e^{-\beta H}$ normalizes the probability density. Integrating the Poisson bracket by parts, with vanishing boundary terms, and using $\{\rho_{\rm eq},B\}_{\rm PB}=\beta\rho_{\rm eq}\dot B$ yields

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\chi_{AB}^{\rm cl}(t)
&=-\Theta(t)\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,\rho_{\rm eq}\{A(t),B(0)\}_{\rm PB}\\
&=\Theta(t)\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,A(t)\{\rho_{\rm eq},B(0)\}_{\rm PB}\\
&=\beta\Theta(t)\langle\delta A(t)\,\delta\dot B(0)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

We used $\langle\dot B\rangle=0$ in equilibrium. The dot means a derivative under $H$; in particular, $\dot A(0)=\{A(0),H\}_{\rm PB}$, evaluated at time zero. An ordinary-product **cross-correlation** is $C_{AB}(t)=\langle\delta A(t)\delta B(0)\rangle$; setting $B=A$ gives the **autocorrelation** $C_A(t)=C_{AA}(t)$. Stationarity gives $\langle\delta A(t)\dot A(0)\rangle=-\mathrm dC_A(t)/\mathrm dt$. The **classical fluctuation–dissipation theorem** therefore is

<div class="math-display">
$$
\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}(t)
=-\beta\Theta(t)\frac{\mathrm d C_A(t)}{\mathrm dt}.
$$
</div>

This equality is the key computational step: the response can be obtained from a correlation measured with no field applied.

## 2. Quantum and Classical FDT

### 2.1 Quantum Green functions and correlation spectra

Let $A$ be Hermitian, so $A^\dagger=A$, and put $\delta A=A-\langle A\rangle$. In quantum mechanics the two products $\delta A(t)\delta A(0)$ and $\delta A(0)\delta A(t)$ need not agree. Define the retarded, greater, and lesser Green functions by

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
G^R_{AA}(t)&=-i\Theta(t)\langle[\delta A(t),\delta A(0)]\rangle,\\
\chi_{AA}(t)&=-G^R_{AA}(t)/\hbar,\\
G^>_{AA}(t)&=-i\langle\delta A(t)\delta A(0)\rangle,\\
G^<_{AA}(t)&=-i\langle\delta A(0)\delta A(t)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The retarded function is the causal difference of the two orderings, which explains its relation to the response $\chi_{AA}$. With the Fourier convention in Section 1, define the greater and lesser spectra:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
G^R_{AA}(t)&=\Theta(t)\bigl[G^>_{AA}(t)-G^<_{AA}(t)\bigr],\\
S_A^>(\omega)&=iG^>_{AA}(\omega)
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta A(t)\delta A(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
S_A^<(\omega)&=iG^<_{AA}(\omega)
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta A(0)\delta A(t)\rangle\,\mathrm dt
=S_A^>(-\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

### 2.2 Deriving quantum FDT

For the canonical equilibrium state, $p_n=e^{-\beta E_n}/Z$ and $H\lvert n\rangle=E_n\lvert n\rangle$. Insert $\sum_m\lvert m\rangle\langle m\rvert=1$ between the two operators. The phase from $A(t)$ is $e^{-i(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar}$, and its Fourier transform is $2\pi\delta(\omega-(E_m-E_n)/\hbar)$, where this $\delta$ is a Dirac delta peak. Consequently,

<div class="math-display">
$$
S_A^>(\omega)=\frac{2\pi}{Z}\sum_{n,m}e^{-\beta E_n}
|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right).
$$
</div>

For negative frequency, swap $n$ and $m$. Hermiticity leaves the squared matrix element unchanged; on the support of the delta function, the new Boltzmann weight is $e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ times the old one:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_A^>(-\omega)
&=\frac{2\pi}{Z}\sum_{n,m}e^{-\beta E_m}
|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right)\\
&=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}S_A^>(\omega),\\
S_A^<(\omega)&=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}S_A^>(\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

This relation is **detailed balance**: positive- and negative-frequency processes have different thermal weights. To obtain the response, let $D(t)=\langle[\delta A(t),\delta A(0)]\rangle$. Stationarity gives $D(-t)=-D(t)$, while $\chi_{AA}(t)=i\Theta(t)D(t)/\hbar$ is real for Hermitian $A$. Taking the imaginary part of its Fourier transform therefore gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\hbar\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega)
&=2i\int_0^\infty\sin(\omega t)D(t)\,\mathrm dt\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}D(t)\,\mathrm dt\\
&=S_A^>(\omega)-S_A^<(\omega)\\
&=(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})S_A^>(\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

This is the **quantum FDT**. It tells us exactly how to obtain the absorptive part of a linear response from an equilibrium correlation, including the unequal probabilities of energy absorption and emission.

### 2.3 Classical FDT and the useful connection

For the symmetrized spectrum $S_A^{\rm sym}=(S_A^{>}+S_A^{<})/2$, divide the sum of the two spectra by their difference. Detailed balance gives $(1+e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})/(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})=\coth(\beta\hbar\omega/2)$, hence

<div class="math-display">
$$
S_A^{\rm sym}(\omega)
=\hbar\coth\!\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)
  \operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega)
\;\xrightarrow{\;\beta\hbar\omega\ll1\;}\;
S_A^{\rm cl}(\omega)
=\frac{2k_{\rm B}T}{\omega}
  \operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}(\omega).
$$
</div>

Here $S_A^{\rm cl}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}C_A(t)\,\mathrm dt$ is the classical equilibrium autocorrelation spectrum. The limit assumes $\beta\hbar\omega\ll1$ and a valid classical description of the dynamics. The same result follows directly from the time-domain classical FDT. Since $C_A(t)=C_A(-t)$, integrate by parts (assuming $C_A(t)$ decays at long times):

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}(\omega)
&=-\beta\int_0^\infty\sin(\omega t)\dot C_A(t)\,\mathrm dt\\
&=\beta\omega\int_0^\infty\cos(\omega t)C_A(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\frac{\beta\omega}{2}S_A^{\rm cl}(\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Thus the classical and quantum formulas are connected, but the latter retains detailed balance and zero-point effects that classical MD cannot supply.

The link works in both directions: equilibrium fluctuations predict absorption, and a response measurement constrains equilibrium fluctuations. Because $\chi(t)=0$ for $t<0$, its Fourier transform is analytic in the upper half of the complex-frequency plane. The Cauchy integral formula then relates its real and imaginary parts, for a response that vanishes sufficiently fast at high frequency:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\operatorname{Re}\chi_{AA}(\omega)=\frac1\pi\mathcal P\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega')}{\omega'-\omega}\,\mathrm d\omega'.
$$
</div>

Here $\mathcal P$ means the principal value: omit a symmetric interval around $\omega'=\omega$ before taking its width to zero. [Kubo's original paper](https://doi.org/10.1143/JPSJ.12.570) and this [FDT review](https://www.zib.de/userpage/donati/stochastics2023/06/references/Kubo1966.pdf) provide further background.

## 3. Property Map: What Do We Correlate?

For the classical transport examples below, assume equilibrium and isotropic three-dimensional response. A *polarization* $\mathbf B_b$ is an accumulated transported quantity; its derivative is the total current $\mathbf J_b=\dot{\mathbf B}_b$. Insert $A=\mathbf J_a$ and $B=\mathbf B_b$ into the cross-response derived in Section 1:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\dot{\mathbf B}_b=\mathbf J_b,\qquad
\chi_{J_{a,x}B_{b,x}}^{\rm cl}(t)
=\beta\Theta(t)\langle\delta J_{a,x}(t)\delta J_{b,x}(0)\rangle.
$$
</div>

The measured current density is $\mathbf j_a=\mathbf J_a/V$. For a steady weak field $f_b$, integrate the response over $t>0$ and divide by $V$. In an isotropic material, the average over $x,y,z$ replaces a single component by one third of the dot product.

For coupled charge and heat transport, $\mathbf J_e$ is total electric charge current and $\mathbf J_Q=\mathbf J_E-\mu_{\rm chem}\mathbf J_N$ is total heat current for a single particle species, where $\mathbf J_E$ and $\mathbf J_N$ are energy and particle currents. The entropy production *per volume* is

<div class="math-display">
$$
\dot s_{\rm vol}
=\mathbf j_e\cdot\frac{\mathbf E}{T}
-\mathbf j_Q\cdot\frac{\nabla T}{T^2}.
$$
</div>

Thus the forces paired with electric and heat currents are $\mathbf E/T$ and $-\nabla T/T^2$. For the heat channel, the temperature gradient is represented in linear response by a thermal field coupled to an energy or heat polarization. With $\mathbf J_a=V\mathbf j_a$, the zero-frequency response takes the form

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{ab}&=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf J_a(t)\cdot\mathbf J_b(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf j_e\\\mathbf j_Q\end{pmatrix}
&=\begin{pmatrix}L_{ee}&L_{eQ}\\L_{Qe}&L_{QQ}\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf E/T\\-\nabla T/T^2\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Equilibrium currents have zero mean, so their $\delta$ symbols have been omitted in $L_{ab}$. The factor $1/k_{\rm B}$ comes from writing the field in terms of the entropy-production force rather than the mechanical field: $\beta f_b=f_b/(k_{\rm B}T)$. In anisotropic materials, retain components $L_{ab}^{\alpha\beta}$ instead of averaging directions.

### 3.1 Self-diffusivity

Apply a small force to one tagged particle: $H'=-F_x x$. Insert measured velocity $A=v_x$, coupled position $B=x$, and $\dot x=v_x$ into the classical cross-response. The steady velocity per force is the mobility $\mu$. To connect it to diffusion, set the particle flux $j=-D\,\partial_x n+\mu nF_x$ to zero in equilibrium; the Boltzmann density $n(x)\propto e^{\beta F_xx}$ then requires $D=\mu/\beta$. Therefore,

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu&=\int_0^\infty\chi_{v_xx}^{\rm cl}(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\beta\int_0^\infty\langle v_x(t)v_x(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
D&=k_{\rm B}T\mu
=\frac13\int_0^\infty\langle\mathbf v(t)\cdot\mathbf v(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

For $\Delta\mathbf r(t)=\int_0^t\mathbf v(s)\,\mathrm ds$, expand the squared integral and use stationarity to combine its two time variables. This gives the equivalent mean-square-displacement formula:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\langle\lVert\Delta\mathbf r(t)\rVert^2\rangle
=2\int_0^t(t-s)\langle\mathbf v(s)\cdot\mathbf v(0)\rangle\,\mathrm ds
\;\Longrightarrow\;
D=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\langle\lVert\Delta\mathbf r(t)\rVert^2\rangle}{6t}.
$$
</div>

### 3.2 Shear viscosity

For shear flow $u_x(y)$, the measured momentum flux is the off-diagonal pressure $P_{xy}$, with constitutive law $\delta P_{xy}=-\eta\,\partial_y u_x$. Set $J_p=V\delta P_{xy}$ and define the accumulated momentum transport (Helfand polarization) by $\dot B_p=J_p$. The conjugate field is the shear rate $f_p=-\partial_y u_x$; insert $A=J_p$, $B=B_p$ into the classical cross-response:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\chi_{J_pB_p}^{\rm cl}(t)&=\beta\Theta(t)
\langle J_p(t)J_p(0)\rangle,\\
\eta&=\frac1V\int_0^\infty\chi_{J_pB_p}^{\rm cl}(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\frac{V}{k_{\rm B}T}\int_0^\infty
\langle\delta P_{xy}(t)\delta P_{xy}(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The same stress autocorrelation is the standard Green–Kubo expression used in [MD viscosity calculations](https://docs.lammps.org/Howto_viscosity.html).

### 3.3 Ionic conductivity

The electric field couples to the total charge dipole $\mathbf P_e$. Its time derivative is the total ionic charge current $\mathbf I$. Insert $A=\mathbf I$, $B=\mathbf P_e$ into Section 1 and divide the current response by $V$; the dot product averages three equivalent directions:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf P_e,\qquad
\mathbf P_e=\sum_a q_a\mathbf r_a,\qquad
\dot{\mathbf P}_e=\mathbf I=\sum_a q_a\mathbf v_a,\\
\sigma_{\rm ion}&=\frac{L_{ee}}{T}
=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}T}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf I(t)\cdot\mathbf I(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\langle\mathbf I(t)\cdot\mathbf I(0)\rangle
&=\sum_{a,b}q_aq_b
\langle\mathbf v_a(t)\cdot\mathbf v_b(0)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

### 3.4 Thermal conductivity

With no coupled charge or particle flow, set $a=b=Q$ in the transport matrix. The thermal force is $-\nabla T/T^2$, while Fourier's law is $\mathbf j_Q=-\kappa\nabla T$. Equating the coefficients gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf j_Q&=L_{QQ}\left(-\frac{\nabla T}{T^2}\right),\qquad
\mathbf J_Q=V\mathbf j_Q,\\
\kappa&=\frac{L_{QQ}}{T^2}
=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}T^2}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf J_Q(t)\cdot\mathbf J_Q(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here $\mathbf J_Q$ is the *total* heat current, not the heat-current density. If charge and heat flow are coupled, eliminate the charge current using the full $2\times2$ matrix before identifying the measured $\kappa$. [MD example](https://docs.lammps.org/compute_heat_flux.html).

### 3.5 Electronic conductivity

For electrons, a spatially uniform vector potential couples to the total current $J_\alpha$. With our Fourier convention, $\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf A^{\rm ext}$ becomes $\mathbf E(\omega)=i\omega\mathbf A^{\rm ext}(\omega)$. Kubo gives the response of the current operator at zero field, $\delta J_\alpha=\chi_{J_\alpha J_\alpha}A_\alpha^{\rm ext}$. The physical current also changes explicitly when the vector potential changes; call that real, instantaneous coefficient $K_{\rm contact}$.

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-J_\alpha A_\alpha^{\rm ext},\qquad
\sigma_{\alpha\alpha}(\omega)
=\frac{\chi_{J_\alpha J_\alpha}(\omega)+K_{\rm contact}}{iV\omega},
\qquad
\mathbf j=\frac{\mathbf J}{V},\\
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{\alpha\alpha}(\omega)
&=\frac{\operatorname{Im}\chi_{J_\alpha J_\alpha}(\omega)}{V\omega}
=\frac{\pi}{V\hbar\omega}\sum_{n,m}(p_n-p_m)
|\langle m|J_\alpha|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right)
\quad(\omega>0).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Dividing by $i\omega$ turns $\operatorname{Im}\chi$ into $\operatorname{Re}\sigma$; the real contact term does not contribute to this finite-frequency absorptive part. The last equality substitutes $A=J_\alpha$ in the spectral result of Section 2, with $p_n=e^{-\beta E_n}/Z$. Band energies and current matrix elements yield the Kubo–Greenwood approximation. The DC value is $\lim_{\omega\to0^+}\operatorname{Re}\sigma(\omega)$ when this limit exists; a singular zero-frequency Drude contribution needs separate treatment. [Optical-properties note](/blog/optical-properties-kubo-dft-gw/).

### 3.6 Thermoelectric response

Use the *off-diagonal* entry $a=e$, $b=Q$ of the transport matrix: it correlates charge current with heat current. At open circuit, solve $\mathbf j_e=0$ for the electric field required to cancel the thermally driven charge flow. For an isotropic material,

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{eQ}&=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf J_e(t)\cdot\mathbf J_Q(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\mathbf j_e&=\frac{L_{ee}}{T}\mathbf E
-\frac{L_{eQ}}{T^2}\nabla T,\\
\mathbf j_e=0&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S\equiv\frac{E}{\nabla T}
=\frac{L_{eQ}}{T L_{ee}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here $S$ uses the convention $S=E/\nabla T$ along the same direction; some voltage conventions insert a minus sign.

### 3.7 Electric or magnetic susceptibility

Choose the moment $A$ conjugate to an applied field $h$ and measure that same moment. Set $A=B$ in the quantum Kubo formula, then use quantum FDT for its absorptive part:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-hA,\qquad A=M_{\rm electric}\ \text{or}\ M_{\rm magnetic},\\
\chi_{AA}(\omega)&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^\infty e^{i\omega t}
\langle[A(t),A(0)]\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega)&=
\frac{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{2\hbar}S_A^>(\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

For a classical *static* field, the zero-frequency response is the time integral of the response kernel. Integrate the time-domain classical FDT, assuming $C_A(t)\to0$:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}(0)
=-\beta\int_0^\infty\dot C_A(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\beta C_A(0)=\beta\operatorname{Var}(A).
$$
</div>

For a total electric dipole $M$, the bulk polarization is $P=M/V$, so $\partial P/\partial E=\chi_{MM}/V$ and the dimensionless electric susceptibility is $\chi_{MM}/(\epsilon_0V)$ in SI units, where $\epsilon_0$ is vacuum permittivity. A magnetic susceptibility uses its corresponding magnetic-field convention.

### 3.8 Infrared absorption

Set $A=B=M$, the total electric dipole along the light polarization. For $E(t)=\operatorname{Re}(E_0e^{-i\omega t})$, where $E_0$ is the complex field amplitude, linear response gives $\delta\langle M(t)\rangle=\operatorname{Re}[\chi_{MM}(\omega)E_0e^{-i\omega t}]$. Since the field transfers power $E(t)\,\mathrm d\langle M(t)\rangle/\mathrm dt$, averaging over one cycle yields

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-E(t)M,\qquad
\delta\langle M\rangle(\omega)=\chi_{MM}(\omega)E(\omega),\\
\overline{P}_{\rm abs}
&=\overline{E(t)\dot M(t)}
=\frac{\omega}{2}|E_0|^2\operatorname{Im}\chi_{MM}(\omega),\\
I_{\rm IR}(\omega)&\propto\omega\operatorname{Im}\chi_{MM}(\omega)
=\frac{\omega(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})}{2\hbar}S_M^>(\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The last equality substitutes the quantum FDT with $A=M$. The proportionality in $I_{\rm IR}$ omits optical geometry and unit factors. In classical MD, use the dipole autocorrelation and classical FDT; harmonic DFPT obtains the dipole's mode matrix elements from effective charges.

### 3.9 Nonresonant Raman scattering

For nonresonant light, the incident field induces a dipole $\mathbf p_{\rm ind}=\boldsymbol\alpha\mathbf E_{\rm in}$. Projecting its scattered component onto the outgoing polarization selects $a=\mathbf e_{\rm out}\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf e_{\rm in}$. Let $\omega=\omega_L-\omega_{\rm out}>0$ be the Stokes shift from incident frequency $\omega_L$. Transitions $n\to m$ then sample the matrix elements of $a$, exactly as in the greater spectrum from Section 2:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'_{\rm light}&=-\tfrac12\mathbf E\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf E,
\qquad a=\mathbf e_{\rm out}\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf e_{\rm in},\\
p_{\rm out}(\omega_{\rm out})
&\propto E_0\!\int\mathrm dt\,
e^{i(\omega_{\rm out}-\omega_L)t}a(t),\\
S_a^>(\omega)&=2\pi\sum_{n,m}p_n
|\langle m|\delta a|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right),\\
I_{\rm Raman}(\omega)&\propto
(\omega_L-\omega)^4\sum_{n,m}p_n|\langle m|\delta a|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right)\\
&=\frac{(\omega_L-\omega)^4}{2\pi}S_a^>(\omega),\\
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{aa}(\omega)&=\frac{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{2\hbar}S_a^>(\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The Fourier component of the induced dipole selects transitions with energy loss $\hbar\omega$; its squared amplitude produces $S_a^>$ above. Dipole-radiated intensity contributes $\omega_{\rm out}^4=(\omega_L-\omega)^4$. The last line substitutes $A=a$ into quantum FDT. Optical geometry and constants are absorbed into the proportionality. [DFPT example](https://quantum-espresso.org/Doc/INPUT_DYNMAT.html).

### 3.10 Neutron or X-ray scattering

For a probe transferring momentum $\mathbf q$, define $\rho_{\mathbf q}=\sum_j e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r_j}$; then $\rho_{-\mathbf q}=\rho_{\mathbf q}^\dagger$. The probe couples to $\rho_{-\mathbf q}$ and measures $\rho_{\mathbf q}$. The inelastic dynamic structure factor is the corresponding greater correlation divided by $2\pi N$, where $N$ is the number of scattering particles:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-f_{\mathbf q}(t)\rho_{-\mathbf q},\qquad
\rho_{\mathbf q}=\sum_j e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r_j},\\
S_{\rm inel}(\mathbf q,\omega)
&=\frac{1}{2\pi N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta\rho_{\mathbf q}(t)\delta\rho_{-\mathbf q}(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
&=\frac1N\sum_{n,m}p_n
|\langle m|\delta\rho_{-\mathbf q}|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right),\\
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{\rho_{\mathbf q}\rho_{-\mathbf q}}(\omega)
&=\frac{\pi N}{\hbar}
(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})S_{\rm inel}(\mathbf q,\omega),\\
\frac{\mathrm d^2\sigma}{\mathrm d\Omega\,\mathrm d\omega}
&\propto S_{\rm inel}(\mathbf q,\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The second line follows by inserting energy eigenstates, as in Section 2. In the weak-scattering (first Born) limit, the same density matrix element gives the probe's transition probability, so its cross section is proportional to $S_{\rm inel}$. Applying detailed balance to $\rho_{\mathbf q},\rho_{-\mathbf q}$ gives spectral difference $2\hbar\operatorname{Im}\chi_{\rho_{\mathbf q}\rho_{-\mathbf q}}$; substituting $S^>=2\pi N S_{\rm inel}$ gives the response line. Probe-dependent factors are omitted. Add the elastic Bragg peak separately; for different atoms, put scattering lengths or form factors inside the density operator.

### 3.11 Phonon or vibrational DOS

Let $N_q$ be the number of unit cells (or sampled wave vectors), $\nu$ a phonon branch, $Q_{\mathbf q\nu}$ a mass-weighted normal coordinate, and $a$ an atom in the full sample. In a classical harmonic system, equipartition gives $\langle\dot Q_{\mathbf q\nu}(0)^2\rangle=k_{\rm B}T$, and harmonic evolution contributes the cosine below. Summing modes and Fourier-transforming gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\dot Q_{\mathbf q\nu}(t)\dot Q_{\mathbf q\nu}(0)\rangle
&=k_{\rm B}T\cos(\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}t),\\
C_v(t)&\equiv\sum_a m_a
\langle\mathbf v_a(t)\cdot\mathbf v_a(0)\rangle
=k_{\rm B}T\sum_{\mathbf q\nu}\cos(\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}t),\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\cos(\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}t)\,\mathrm dt
&=\pi\bigl[\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})
+\delta(\omega+\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})\bigr],\\
g_{\rm ph}(\omega)
&=\frac1{N_q}\sum_{\mathbf q\nu}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})
=\frac{1}{\pi N_qk_{\rm B}T}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}C_v(t)\,\mathrm dt
\quad(\omega>0).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The positive-frequency delta functions count modes per unit cell; they are not a current-response coefficient. The equality with an MD velocity spectrum assumes classical harmonic motion; anharmonic motion broadens the peaks. [Phonon calculation](https://www.quantum-espresso.org/Doc/INPUT_MATDYN.html); [MD spectrum](https://manual.gromacs.org/current/onlinehelp/gmx-dos.html).

### 3.12 Electronic DOS

An electronic DOS counts states at energy $E$ rather than the response of a current to a field. Use the **fermionic one-particle** retarded Green function, with electron annihilation operator $c_i$, creation operator $c_j^\dagger$, and anticommutator $\{c_i,c_j^\dagger\}=c_ic_j^\dagger+c_j^\dagger c_i$. Here the energy transform is $G^R(E)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iEt/\hbar}G^R(t)\,\mathrm dt$. For a noninteracting band of energy $\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}$, the pole identity $-\operatorname{Im}(x+i0^+)^{-1}/\pi=\delta(x)$ gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
G^R_{ij}(t)&=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t)
\langle\{c_i(t),c_j^\dagger(0)\}\rangle,\\
G^R_{n\mathbf k}(E)&=\frac{1}{E-\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}+i0^+},
\qquad
-\frac1\pi\operatorname{Im}G^R_{n\mathbf k}(E)
=\delta(E-\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}),\\
g_{\rm KS}(E)&=\frac1{N_k}\sum_{n\mathbf k}
\delta(E-\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}),\\
g(E)&=-\frac{1}{\pi N_k}\sum_{\mathbf k}
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Tr}_nG^R(\mathbf k,E).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here $N_k$ is the number of sampled wave vectors, $\operatorname{Tr}_n$ sums the band or orbital indices, and $g_{\rm KS}$ is the Kohn–Sham DOS obtained from DFT band energies. The last line is the interacting spectral DOS when $G^R$ includes a self-energy. The one-particle anticommutator Green function is different from the commutator response Green function in Section 2; the DOS is therefore related to spectral analysis, not another Green–Kubo coefficient. [DFT DOS example](https://quantum-espresso.org/Doc/pp_user_guide/node9.html).

## 4. MD, DFT, and the Practical Workflow

**MD:** record the observable named in the formula, subtract its equilibrium mean where required, and estimate $C_{AB}(t)$ by averaging over many starting times. Integrate it for a zero-frequency transport coefficient or Fourier-transform it for a spectrum. Check that a running transport integral reaches a stable range; check time step, trajectory length, finite-size effects, and uncertainty before interpreting a spectrum. [GROMACS' correlation-function guide](https://manual.gromacs.org/current/reference-manual/analysis/correlation-function.html) gives practical examples.

**DFT and DFPT:** ground-state DFT supplies electronic states and forces; DFPT supplies harmonic modes, effective charges, and response tensors. To use a time-correlation formula for nuclear motion, generate a trajectory with ab initio MD. To use a spectral formula, assemble transitions or mode matrix elements from the calculated states. A static DFT result alone is not a time correlation.

**Before using a formula:** write the coupling $H'=-fB$, identify the measured $A$, and ask whether the target is a response, a scattering spectrum, or a mode count. Then choose the time integral or finite-frequency spectrum and check whether classical or quantum statistics apply. Those choices determine the correlation and its normalization.
