# From Fluctuations to Materials Properties: Kubo, FDT, and Correlations

An equilibrium material still has fluctuating velocities, currents, dipoles, and spins. We will derive how a weak applied field probes those fluctuations, then use the result to calculate transport coefficients and spectra.

The recurring choice is the observable: velocity for diffusion, stress for viscosity, currents for conductivity, and dipole or polarizability for optical spectra. Phonon and electron densities of states (DOS) are included as related spectra; they count modes or one-particle states rather than measure a transport response.

## 1. From a Weak Field to a Response Function

Let <span markdown="0">\(H\)</span> be the unperturbed Hamiltonian and <span markdown="0">\(T\)</span> the absolute temperature. Write <span markdown="0">\(k_{\rm B}\)</span> for Boltzmann's constant, <span markdown="0">\(\hbar\)</span> for the reduced Planck constant, <span markdown="0">\(\beta=1/(k_{\rm B}T)\)</span>, and <span markdown="0">\(\langle O\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H}O)/Z\)</span> with <span markdown="0">\(Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}\)</span>. An operator's time dependence, <span markdown="0">\(A(t)=e^{iHt/\hbar}Ae^{-iHt/\hbar}\)</span>, is generated by <span markdown="0">\(H\)</span>. We apply a small field <span markdown="0">\(f(t)\)</span> through <span markdown="0">\(H'(t)=-f(t)B\)</span>: <span markdown="0">\(B\)</span> is the quantity coupled to the field, and <span markdown="0">\(A\)</span> is the quantity measured. Write <span markdown="0">\(\delta\langle A\rangle=\langle A\rangle_f-\langle A\rangle_0\)</span> for the change caused by the field; separately, <span markdown="0">\(\delta A=A-\langle A\rangle_0\)</span> denotes an equilibrium fluctuation.

To first order in <span markdown="0">\(f\)</span>, expand the interaction-picture evolution operator, which describes evolution due to <span markdown="0">\(H'\)</span> after the evolution due to <span markdown="0">\(H\)</span> has been factored out: <span markdown="0">\(U_I=1-(i/\hbar)\int_{-\infty}^t H'_I(s)\,\mathrm ds+O(f^2)\)</span>. Inserting it into <span markdown="0">\(\langle U_I^\dagger A(t)U_I\rangle\)</span> gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta\langle A(t)\rangle
&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{t}f(s)
  \langle[A(t),B(s)]\rangle\,\mathrm ds,\\
\chi_{AB}(t-s)
&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t-s)
  \langle[A(t),B(s)]\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here <span markdown="0">\([A,B]=AB-BA\)</span>, <span markdown="0">\(\Theta(t)=1\)</span> for <span markdown="0">\(t>0\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(0\)</span> for <span markdown="0">\(t<0\)</span>, and equilibrium time-translation symmetry makes the average depend only on <span markdown="0">\(t-s\)</span>. Thus the **quantum Kubo formula** can be written

<div class="math-display">
$$
\delta\langle A(t)\rangle
=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\chi_{AB}(t-s)f(s)\,\mathrm ds,
\qquad
\chi_{AB}(t)
=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t)
\left\langle[A(t),B(0)]\right\rangle_{\rm eq}.
$$
</div>

The first subscript of <span markdown="0">\(\chi_{AB}\)</span> names the measured observable; the second names the field's conjugate observable. For example, <span markdown="0">\(H'=-EM\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(A=M\)</span> give the dipole response <span markdown="0">\(\chi_{MM}\)</span>. Causality is encoded by <span markdown="0">\(\Theta(t)\)</span>. Throughout, <span markdown="0">\(\omega\)</span> is angular frequency and <span markdown="0">\(F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}F(t)\,\mathrm dt\)</span>; the frequency-domain response is <span markdown="0">\(\delta\langle A\rangle(\omega)=\chi_{AB}(\omega)f(\omega)\)</span>.

For a real sinusoidal field, <span markdown="0">\(\operatorname{Re}\chi_{AA}(\omega)\)</span> gives the part of the response in phase with the field and <span markdown="0">\(\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega)\)</span> gives the out-of-phase part. The latter produces absorbed power, as derived in the infrared example below.

For classical dynamics, positions and momenta replace operators. Write <span markdown="0">\(\Gamma=(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N;\mathbf p_1,\ldots,\mathbf p_N)\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(\mathrm d\Gamma=\prod_i\mathrm d^3r_i\,\mathrm d^3p_i\)</span>. The **Poisson bracket** (PB) and the unperturbed time derivative are

<div class="math-display">
$$
\{F,G\}_{\rm PB}
=\sum_{i,\alpha}\left(
\frac{\partial F}{\partial r_{i\alpha}}
\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_{i\alpha}}
-\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{i\alpha}}
\frac{\partial G}{\partial r_{i\alpha}}\right),
\qquad
\dot B=\frac{\mathrm d B}{\mathrm dt}=\{B,H\}_{\rm PB}.
$$
</div>

The sum runs over particles <span markdown="0">\(i\)</span> and Cartesian directions <span markdown="0">\(\alpha\)</span>. Under <span markdown="0">\(H+H'\)</span>, Hamilton's equation for any function <span markdown="0">\(F\)</span> of the positions and momenta is <span markdown="0">\(\mathrm dF/\mathrm dt=\{F,H\}_{\rm PB}-f(t)\{F,B\}_{\rm PB}\)</span>.

Imagine that the field acts only during a short interval <span markdown="0">\(\mathrm ds\)</span> at time <span markdown="0">\(s\)</span>. The part of Hamilton's equation caused by the field changes <span markdown="0">\(F\)</span> by <span markdown="0">\(-f(s)\,\mathrm ds\,\{F,B\}_{\rm PB}\)</span>. To find the effect on a measurement at a later time <span markdown="0">\(t\)</span>, choose <span markdown="0">\(F=A_0(t-s)\)</span>, viewed as a function of the positions and momenta at <span markdown="0">\(s\)</span>: it gives the value that <span markdown="0">\(A\)</span> will have after another <span markdown="0">\(t-s\)</span> of motion under <span markdown="0">\(H\)</span> alone. For this short interval, take <span markdown="0">\(s\)</span> as time zero. Then

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta_s A(t)
&=-f(s)\,\mathrm ds\,\{A_0(t-s),B(0)\}_{\rm PB},\\
\delta\langle A(t)\rangle_{\rm cl}
&=-\int_{-\infty}^t f(s)
  \langle\{A_0(t-s),B(0)\}_{\rm PB}\rangle\,\mathrm ds.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

For a familiar example, <span markdown="0">\(B=x\)</span> means <span markdown="0">\(H'=-f(s)x\)</span>: the short pulse adds <span markdown="0">\(f(s)\,\mathrm ds\)</span> to the momentum, which changes the position measured later. The Poisson bracket gives that later change for any choice of <span markdown="0">\(A\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(B\)</span>.

Adding the effects of all pulses before <span markdown="0">\(t\)</span> and averaging over equilibrium states gives the integral. Equilibrium is unchanged by shifting the time origin, so the average depends only on <span markdown="0">\(t-s\)</span>. We use field-free motion inside the bracket because its field correction would be multiplied by <span markdown="0">\(f(s)\)</span> and would be second order. Dropping the subscript <span markdown="0">\(0\)</span> for field-free quantities, we identify the response kernel:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\delta\langle A(t)\rangle_{\rm cl}
=\int_{-\infty}^t\chi_{AB}^{\rm cl}(t-s)f(s)\,\mathrm ds,
\qquad
\chi_{AB}^{\rm cl}(t)
=-\Theta(t)\langle\{A(t),B(0)\}_{\rm PB}\rangle.
$$
</div>

The superscript “cl” means classical. Set <span markdown="0">\(\rho_{\rm eq}(\Gamma)=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta H(\Gamma)}\)</span>, where <span markdown="0">\(Z=\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,e^{-\beta H}\)</span> normalizes the equilibrium density. Phase-space integration by parts gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,\rho_{\rm eq}\{F,B\}_{\rm PB}
&=-\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,F\sum_{i,\alpha}
\left[
\partial_{r_{i\alpha}}(\rho_{\rm eq}\partial_{p_{i\alpha}}B)
-\partial_{p_{i\alpha}}(\rho_{\rm eq}\partial_{r_{i\alpha}}B)
\right]\\
&=-\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,F\{\rho_{\rm eq},B\}_{\rm PB}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here <span markdown="0">\(F\)</span> can be any phase-space function. The two terms containing mixed second derivatives of <span markdown="0">\(B\)</span> cancel; boundary terms vanish for periodic coordinates or sufficiently decaying densities. For the next step, use the chain rule on the Boltzmann density and then Hamilton's equation:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\{\rho_{\rm eq},B\}_{\rm PB}
=-\beta\rho_{\rm eq}\{H,B\}_{\rm PB}
=\beta\rho_{\rm eq}\{B,H\}_{\rm PB}
=\beta\rho_{\rm eq}\dot B.
$$
</div>

Putting these identities into the response kernel gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\chi_{AB}^{\rm cl}(t)
&=-\Theta(t)\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,\rho_{\rm eq}\{A(t),B(0)\}_{\rm PB}\\
&=\Theta(t)\int\mathrm d\Gamma\,A(t)\{\rho_{\rm eq},B(0)\}_{\rm PB}\\
&=\beta\Theta(t)\langle A(t)\dot B(0)\rangle\\
&=\beta\Theta(t)\langle\delta A(t)\,\delta\dot B(0)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The last line uses stationarity: <span markdown="0">\(\langle\dot B\rangle=\mathrm d\langle B\rangle/\mathrm dt=0\)</span>. Subtracting <span markdown="0">\(\langle A\rangle\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(\langle\dot B\rangle\)</span> therefore leaves the product's average unchanged.

The dot means a derivative under <span markdown="0">\(H\)</span>; for example, <span markdown="0">\(\dot B(0)=\{B(0),H\}_{\rm PB}\)</span>. Define the **cross-correlation** <span markdown="0">\(C_{AB}(t)=\langle\delta A(t)\delta B(0)\rangle\)</span>. In equilibrium, shifting both times by the same amount leaves this average unchanged. Differentiating that statement gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
0=\left.\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}
\langle\delta A(t+s)\delta B(s)\rangle\right|_{s=0}
=\frac{\mathrm d C_{AB}(t)}{\mathrm dt}
+\langle\delta A(t)\delta\dot B(0)\rangle.
$$
</div>

Putting this into the response above gives the general **classical fluctuation–dissipation theorem**:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\chi_{AB}^{\rm cl}(t)
=-\beta\Theta(t)\frac{\mathrm d C_{AB}(t)}{\mathrm dt}.
$$
</div>

Setting <span markdown="0">\(B=A\)</span> gives the autocorrelation <span markdown="0">\(C_A=C_{AA}\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}=-\beta\Theta\,\mathrm dC_A/\mathrm dt\)</span> as a special case. The response can thus be obtained from a correlation measured with no field applied.

## 2. Quantum and Classical FDT

### 2.1 Retarded Green function

For observables <span markdown="0">\(A\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(B\)</span>, define <span markdown="0">\(\delta A=A-\langle A\rangle\)</span> and likewise for <span markdown="0">\(B\)</span>. The **retarded Green function** uses the commutator and vanishes before the perturbation acts:

<div class="math-display">
$$
G^R_{AB}(t)=-i\Theta(t)\langle[\delta A(t),\delta B(0)]\rangle,
\qquad \chi_{AB}(t)=-\frac{G^R_{AB}(t)}{\hbar}.
$$
</div>

The second equality follows directly from the Kubo formula for <span markdown="0">\(H'=-fB\)</span>. Subtracting equilibrium means leaves a commutator unchanged. To derive FDT, set <span markdown="0">\(B=A=A^\dagger\)</span> and write its ordered *quantum* correlation as

<div class="math-display">
$$
C_A(t)=\langle\delta A(t)\delta A(0)\rangle.
$$
</div>

Operator order matters: <span markdown="0">\(\delta A(t)\delta A(0)\)</span> need not equal <span markdown="0">\(\delta A(0)\delta A(t)\)</span>. In equilibrium, <span markdown="0">\(\rho_{\rm eq}\)</span> commutes with <span markdown="0">\(H\)</span>. Cyclicity of the trace then shows why shifting *both* times by <span markdown="0">\(s\)</span> leaves an average unchanged:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\delta A(t_1+s)\delta A(t_2+s)\rangle
&=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho_{\rm eq}e^{iHs/\hbar}
\delta A(t_1)\delta A(t_2)e^{-iHs/\hbar}\right]\\
&=\langle\delta A(t_1)\delta A(t_2)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The last equality uses <span markdown="0">\(e^{-iHs/\hbar}\rho_{\rm eq}e^{iHs/\hbar}=\rho_{\rm eq}\)</span>. Taking <span markdown="0">\((t_1,t_2,s)=(0,t,-t)\)</span> gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\delta A(0)\delta A(t)\rangle
&=\langle\delta A(-t)\delta A(0)\rangle=C_A(-t),\\
D(t)&\equiv\langle[\delta A(t),\delta A(0)]\rangle
=C_A(t)-C_A(-t),\\
D(-t)&=C_A(-t)-C_A(t)=-D(t).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Thus the oddness of the commutator follows from stationarity and the definition of a commutator. We next use energy eigenstates to turn this time-domain statement into the frequency-domain FDT.

### 2.2 Deriving quantum FDT from energy eigenstates

Write <span markdown="0">\(H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(p_n=e^{-\beta E_n}/Z\)</span>. Inserting <span markdown="0">\(\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|=1\)</span> between the two factors of <span markdown="0">\(\delta A\)</span> gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_A(t)
&=\sum_{n,m}p_n\langle n|\delta A|m\rangle
\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle e^{-i(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar}\\
&=\sum_{n,m}p_n|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2
e^{-i(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar},\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}C_A(t)\,\mathrm dt
&=2\pi\sum_{n,m}p_n|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The first equality uses <span markdown="0">\(A(t)=e^{iHt/\hbar}Ae^{-iHt/\hbar}\)</span>; the second uses Hermiticity. The last line Fourier-transforms <span markdown="0">\(e^{-i(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar}\)</span> into a Dirac delta peak. For the reversed time ordering <span markdown="0">\(C_A(-t)=\langle\delta A(0)\delta A(t)\rangle\)</span>, exchange <span markdown="0">\(n\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(m\)</span> in the same sum:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}C_A(-t)\,\mathrm dt
&=2\pi\sum_{n,m}p_m|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right)\\
&=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}C_A(t)\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

In the last step, the delta peak sets <span markdown="0">\(E_m-E_n=\hbar\omega\)</span>, so <span markdown="0">\(p_m/p_n=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\)</span>. This is **detailed balance**.

Now use the same energy-state sum in <span markdown="0">\(D(t)=C_A(t)-C_A(-t)\)</span>. Exchanging the indices in the second term changes its weight from <span markdown="0">\(p_n\)</span> to <span markdown="0">\(p_m\)</span>. Fourier-transforming the retarded Green function with a small damping factor <span markdown="0">\(e^{-\eta t}\)</span>, then taking <span markdown="0">\(\eta\to0^+\)</span>, gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
D(t)&=\sum_{n,m}(p_n-p_m)|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2
e^{-i(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar},\\
G^R_{AA}(\omega)
&=\sum_{n,m}\frac{(p_n-p_m)|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2}
{\omega-(E_m-E_n)/\hbar+i0^+}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here <span markdown="0">\(-i\int_0^\infty e^{ixt-\eta t}\,\mathrm dt=(x+i\eta)^{-1}\)</span>. Using <span markdown="0">\(\operatorname{Im}(x+i0^+)^{-1}=-\pi\delta(x)\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(\chi_{AA}=-G^R_{AA}/\hbar\)</span> gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\hbar\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega)
&=2\pi\sum_{n,m}(p_n-p_m)|\langle m|\delta A|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right)\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\bigl[C_A(t)-C_A(-t)\bigr]\,\mathrm dt\\
&=\bigl(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\bigr)
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta A(t)\delta A(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

This is the **quantum FDT**: the imaginary part of the response is the difference between the two time orderings of an equilibrium correlation, with detailed balance fixing their ratio.

### 2.3 Classical FDT and the useful connection

The symmetrized correlation averages the two operator orderings. Their Fourier transforms have the ratio <span markdown="0">\(e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\)</span>, so divide their sum by their difference in the quantum FDT. Since <span markdown="0">\((1+e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})/(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})=\coth(\beta\hbar\omega/2)\)</span>, this gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\frac12\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\{\delta A(t),\delta A(0)\}\rangle\,\mathrm dt
=\hbar\coth\!\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega).
$$
</div>

Here the braces are an **anticommutator**, <span markdown="0">\(\{X,Y\}=XY+YX\)</span>, not the classical Poisson bracket. When <span markdown="0">\(\beta\hbar\omega\ll1\)</span> and the dynamics admit a classical description, <span markdown="0">\(\coth(\beta\hbar\omega/2)\simeq2/(\beta\hbar\omega)\)</span>. With the classical autocorrelation <span markdown="0">\(C_A^{\rm cl}(t)=\langle\delta A(t)\delta A(0)\rangle_{\rm cl}\)</span>, the limit is

<div class="math-display">
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}C_A^{\rm cl}(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\frac{2k_{\rm B}T}{\omega}\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}(\omega).
$$
</div>

The same result follows directly from the time-domain classical FDT. Since <span markdown="0">\(C_A^{\rm cl}(t)=C_A^{\rm cl}(-t)\)</span>, integrate by parts (assuming the correlation decays at long times):

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}(\omega)
&=-\beta\int_0^\infty\sin(\omega t)
\frac{\mathrm d C_A^{\rm cl}(t)}{\mathrm dt}\,\mathrm dt\\
&=\beta\omega\int_0^\infty\cos(\omega t)C_A^{\rm cl}(t)\,\mathrm dt\\
&=\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
e^{i\omega t}C_A^{\rm cl}(t)\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Thus the classical and quantum formulas are connected, but the latter retains detailed balance and zero-point effects that classical MD cannot supply.

The link works in both directions: equilibrium fluctuations predict absorption, and a response measurement constrains equilibrium fluctuations. Because <span markdown="0">\(\chi(t)=0\)</span> for <span markdown="0">\(t<0\)</span>, its Fourier transform is analytic in the upper half of the complex-frequency plane. The Cauchy integral formula then relates its real and imaginary parts, for a response that vanishes sufficiently fast at high frequency:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\operatorname{Re}\chi_{AA}(\omega)=\frac1\pi\mathcal P\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega')}{\omega'-\omega}\,\mathrm d\omega'.
$$
</div>

Here <span markdown="0">\(\mathcal P\)</span> means the principal value: omit a symmetric interval around <span markdown="0">\(\omega'=\omega\)</span> before taking its width to zero. [Kubo's original paper](https://doi.org/10.1143/JPSJ.12.570) and this [FDT review](https://www.zib.de/userpage/donati/stochastics2023/06/references/Kubo1966.pdf) provide further background.

## 3. Property Map: What Do We Correlate?

For the classical transport examples below, assume equilibrium and isotropic three-dimensional response. A *polarization* <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf B_b\)</span> is an accumulated transported quantity; its derivative is the total current <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf J_b=\dot{\mathbf B}_b\)</span>. Insert <span markdown="0">\(A=\mathbf J_a\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(B=\mathbf B_b\)</span> into the cross-response derived in Section 1:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\dot{\mathbf B}_b=\mathbf J_b,\qquad
\chi_{J_{a,x}B_{b,x}}^{\rm cl}(t)
=\beta\Theta(t)\langle\delta J_{a,x}(t)\delta J_{b,x}(0)\rangle.
$$
</div>

The measured current density is <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf j_a=\mathbf J_a/V\)</span>. For a steady weak field <span markdown="0">\(f_b\)</span>, integrate the response over <span markdown="0">\(t>0\)</span> and divide by <span markdown="0">\(V\)</span>. In an isotropic material, the average over <span markdown="0">\(x,y,z\)</span> replaces a single component by one third of the dot product.

For coupled charge and heat transport, <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf J_e\)</span> is total electric charge current and <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf J_Q=\mathbf J_E-\mu_{\rm chem}\mathbf J_N\)</span> is total heat current for a single particle species, where <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf J_E\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf J_N\)</span> are energy and particle currents. The entropy production *per volume* is

<div class="math-display">
$$
\dot s_{\rm vol}
=\mathbf j_e\cdot\frac{\mathbf E}{T}
-\mathbf j_Q\cdot\frac{\nabla T}{T^2}.
$$
</div>

Thus the forces paired with electric and heat currents are <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf E/T\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(-\nabla T/T^2\)</span>. For the heat channel, the temperature gradient is represented in linear response by a thermal field coupled to an energy or heat polarization. With <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf J_a=V\mathbf j_a\)</span>, the zero-frequency response takes the form

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{ab}&=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf J_a(t)\cdot\mathbf J_b(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf j_e\\\mathbf j_Q\end{pmatrix}
&=\begin{pmatrix}L_{ee}&L_{eQ}\\L_{Qe}&L_{QQ}\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf E/T\\-\nabla T/T^2\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Equilibrium currents have zero mean, so their <span markdown="0">\(\delta\)</span> symbols have been omitted in <span markdown="0">\(L_{ab}\)</span>. The factor <span markdown="0">\(1/k_{\rm B}\)</span> comes from writing the field in terms of the entropy-production force rather than the mechanical field: <span markdown="0">\(\beta f_b=f_b/(k_{\rm B}T)\)</span>. In anisotropic materials, retain components <span markdown="0">\(L_{ab}^{\alpha\beta}\)</span> instead of averaging directions.

### 3.1 Self-diffusivity

Apply a small force to one tagged particle: <span markdown="0">\(H'=-F_x x\)</span>. Insert measured velocity <span markdown="0">\(A=v_x\)</span>, coupled position <span markdown="0">\(B=x\)</span>, and <span markdown="0">\(\dot x=v_x\)</span> into the classical cross-response. The steady velocity per force is the mobility <span markdown="0">\(\mu\)</span>. To connect it to diffusion, set the particle flux <span markdown="0">\(j=-D\,\partial_x n+\mu nF_x\)</span> to zero in equilibrium; the Boltzmann density <span markdown="0">\(n(x)\propto e^{\beta F_xx}\)</span> then requires <span markdown="0">\(D=\mu/\beta\)</span>. Therefore,

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu&=\int_0^\infty\chi_{v_xx}^{\rm cl}(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\beta\int_0^\infty\langle v_x(t)v_x(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
D&=k_{\rm B}T\mu
=\frac13\int_0^\infty\langle\mathbf v(t)\cdot\mathbf v(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

For <span markdown="0">\(\Delta\mathbf r(t)=\int_0^t\mathbf v(s)\,\mathrm ds\)</span>, expand the squared integral and use stationarity to combine its two time variables. This gives the equivalent mean-square-displacement formula:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\langle\lVert\Delta\mathbf r(t)\rVert^2\rangle
=2\int_0^t(t-s)\langle\mathbf v(s)\cdot\mathbf v(0)\rangle\,\mathrm ds
\;\Longrightarrow\;
D=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\langle\lVert\Delta\mathbf r(t)\rVert^2\rangle}{6t}.
$$
</div>

### 3.2 Shear viscosity

For shear flow <span markdown="0">\(u_x(y)\)</span>, the measured momentum flux is the off-diagonal pressure <span markdown="0">\(P_{xy}\)</span>, with constitutive law <span markdown="0">\(\delta P_{xy}=-\eta\,\partial_y u_x\)</span>. Set <span markdown="0">\(J_p=V\delta P_{xy}\)</span> and define the accumulated momentum transport (Helfand polarization) by <span markdown="0">\(\dot B_p=J_p\)</span>. The conjugate field is the shear rate <span markdown="0">\(f_p=-\partial_y u_x\)</span>; insert <span markdown="0">\(A=J_p\)</span>, <span markdown="0">\(B=B_p\)</span> into the classical cross-response:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\chi_{J_pB_p}^{\rm cl}(t)&=\beta\Theta(t)
\langle J_p(t)J_p(0)\rangle,\\
\eta&=\frac1V\int_0^\infty\chi_{J_pB_p}^{\rm cl}(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\frac{V}{k_{\rm B}T}\int_0^\infty
\langle\delta P_{xy}(t)\delta P_{xy}(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The same stress autocorrelation is the standard Green–Kubo expression used in [MD viscosity calculations](https://docs.lammps.org/Howto_viscosity.html).

### 3.3 Ionic conductivity

The electric field couples to the total charge dipole <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf P_e\)</span>. Its time derivative is the total ionic charge current <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf I\)</span>. Insert <span markdown="0">\(A=\mathbf I\)</span>, <span markdown="0">\(B=\mathbf P_e\)</span> into Section 1 and divide the current response by <span markdown="0">\(V\)</span>; the dot product averages three equivalent directions:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf P_e,\qquad
\mathbf P_e=\sum_a q_a\mathbf r_a,\qquad
\dot{\mathbf P}_e=\mathbf I=\sum_a q_a\mathbf v_a,\\
\sigma_{\rm ion}&=\frac{L_{ee}}{T}
=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}T}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf I(t)\cdot\mathbf I(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\langle\mathbf I(t)\cdot\mathbf I(0)\rangle
&=\sum_{a,b}q_aq_b
\langle\mathbf v_a(t)\cdot\mathbf v_b(0)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

### 3.4 Thermal conductivity

With no coupled charge or particle flow, set <span markdown="0">\(a=b=Q\)</span> in the transport matrix. The thermal force is <span markdown="0">\(-\nabla T/T^2\)</span>, while Fourier's law is <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf j_Q=-\kappa\nabla T\)</span>. Equating the coefficients gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf j_Q&=L_{QQ}\left(-\frac{\nabla T}{T^2}\right),\qquad
\mathbf J_Q=V\mathbf j_Q,\\
\kappa&=\frac{L_{QQ}}{T^2}
=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}T^2}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf J_Q(t)\cdot\mathbf J_Q(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf J_Q\)</span> is the *total* heat current, not the heat-current density. If charge and heat flow are coupled, eliminate the charge current using the full <span markdown="0">\(2\times2\)</span> matrix before identifying the measured <span markdown="0">\(\kappa\)</span>. [MD example](https://docs.lammps.org/compute_heat_flux.html).

### 3.5 Electronic conductivity

For electrons, a spatially uniform vector potential couples to the total current <span markdown="0">\(J_\alpha\)</span>. With our Fourier convention, <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf A^{\rm ext}\)</span> becomes <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf E(\omega)=i\omega\mathbf A^{\rm ext}(\omega)\)</span>. Kubo gives the response of the current operator at zero field, <span markdown="0">\(\delta J_\alpha=\chi_{J_\alpha J_\alpha}A_\alpha^{\rm ext}\)</span>. The physical current also changes explicitly when the vector potential changes; call that real, instantaneous coefficient <span markdown="0">\(K_{\rm contact}\)</span>.

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-J_\alpha A_\alpha^{\rm ext},\qquad
\sigma_{\alpha\alpha}(\omega)
=\frac{\chi_{J_\alpha J_\alpha}(\omega)+K_{\rm contact}}{iV\omega},
\qquad
\mathbf j=\frac{\mathbf J}{V},\\
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{\alpha\alpha}(\omega)
&=\frac{\operatorname{Im}\chi_{J_\alpha J_\alpha}(\omega)}{V\omega}
=\frac{\pi}{V\hbar\omega}\sum_{n,m}(p_n-p_m)
|\langle m|J_\alpha|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right)
\quad(\omega>0).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Dividing by <span markdown="0">\(i\omega\)</span> turns <span markdown="0">\(\operatorname{Im}\chi\)</span> into <span markdown="0">\(\operatorname{Re}\sigma\)</span>; the real contact term does not contribute to this finite-frequency absorptive part. The last equality substitutes <span markdown="0">\(A=J_\alpha\)</span> in the spectral result of Section 2, with <span markdown="0">\(p_n=e^{-\beta E_n}/Z\)</span>. Band energies and current matrix elements yield the Kubo–Greenwood approximation. The DC value is <span markdown="0">\(\lim_{\omega\to0^+}\operatorname{Re}\sigma(\omega)\)</span> when this limit exists; a singular zero-frequency Drude contribution needs separate treatment. [Optical-properties note](/blog/optical-properties-kubo-dft-gw/).

### 3.6 Thermoelectric response

Use the *off-diagonal* entry <span markdown="0">\(a=e\)</span>, <span markdown="0">\(b=Q\)</span> of the transport matrix: it correlates charge current with heat current. At open circuit, solve <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf j_e=0\)</span> for the electric field required to cancel the thermally driven charge flow. For an isotropic material,

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{eQ}&=\frac{1}{3Vk_{\rm B}}\int_0^\infty
\langle\mathbf J_e(t)\cdot\mathbf J_Q(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\mathbf j_e&=\frac{L_{ee}}{T}\mathbf E
-\frac{L_{eQ}}{T^2}\nabla T,\\
\mathbf j_e=0&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S\equiv\frac{E}{\nabla T}
=\frac{L_{eQ}}{T L_{ee}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here <span markdown="0">\(S\)</span> uses the convention <span markdown="0">\(S=E/\nabla T\)</span> along the same direction; some voltage conventions insert a minus sign.

### 3.7 Electric or magnetic susceptibility

Choose the moment <span markdown="0">\(A\)</span> conjugate to an applied field <span markdown="0">\(h\)</span> and measure that same moment. Set <span markdown="0">\(A=B\)</span> in the quantum Kubo formula, then use quantum FDT for its absorptive part:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-hA,\qquad A=M_{\rm electric}\ \text{or}\ M_{\rm magnetic},\\
\chi_{AA}(\omega)&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^\infty e^{i\omega t}
\langle[A(t),A(0)]\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{AA}(\omega)&=
\frac{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{2\hbar}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta A(t)\delta A(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

For a classical *static* field, the zero-frequency response is the time integral of the response kernel. Integrate the time-domain classical FDT, assuming <span markdown="0">\(C_A(t)\to0\)</span>:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\chi_{AA}^{\rm cl}(0)
=-\beta\int_0^\infty\dot C_A(t)\,\mathrm dt
=\beta C_A(0)=\beta\operatorname{Var}(A).
$$
</div>

For a total electric dipole <span markdown="0">\(M\)</span>, the bulk polarization is <span markdown="0">\(P=M/V\)</span>, so <span markdown="0">\(\partial P/\partial E=\chi_{MM}/V\)</span> and the dimensionless electric susceptibility is <span markdown="0">\(\chi_{MM}/(\epsilon_0V)\)</span> in SI units, where <span markdown="0">\(\epsilon_0\)</span> is vacuum permittivity. A magnetic susceptibility uses its corresponding magnetic-field convention.

### 3.8 Infrared absorption

Set <span markdown="0">\(A=B=M\)</span>, the total electric dipole along the light polarization. For <span markdown="0">\(E(t)=\operatorname{Re}(E_0e^{-i\omega t})\)</span>, where <span markdown="0">\(E_0\)</span> is the complex field amplitude, linear response gives <span markdown="0">\(\delta\langle M(t)\rangle=\operatorname{Re}[\chi_{MM}(\omega)E_0e^{-i\omega t}]\)</span>. Since the field transfers power <span markdown="0">\(E(t)\,\mathrm d\langle M(t)\rangle/\mathrm dt\)</span>, averaging over one cycle yields

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-E(t)M,\qquad
\delta\langle M\rangle(\omega)=\chi_{MM}(\omega)E(\omega),\\
\overline{P}_{\rm abs}
&=\overline{E(t)\dot M(t)}
=\frac{\omega}{2}|E_0|^2\operatorname{Im}\chi_{MM}(\omega),\\
I_{\rm IR}(\omega)&\propto\omega\operatorname{Im}\chi_{MM}(\omega)
=\frac{\omega(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})}{2\hbar}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta M(t)\delta M(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The last equality substitutes the quantum FDT with <span markdown="0">\(A=M\)</span>. The proportionality in <span markdown="0">\(I_{\rm IR}\)</span> omits optical geometry and unit factors. In classical MD, use the dipole autocorrelation and classical FDT; harmonic DFPT obtains the dipole's mode matrix elements from effective charges.

### 3.9 Nonresonant Raman scattering

For nonresonant light, the incident field induces a dipole <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf p_{\rm ind}=\boldsymbol\alpha\mathbf E_{\rm in}\)</span>. Projecting its scattered component onto the outgoing polarization selects <span markdown="0">\(a=\mathbf e_{\rm out}\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf e_{\rm in}\)</span>. Let <span markdown="0">\(\omega=\omega_L-\omega_{\rm out}>0\)</span> be the Stokes shift from incident frequency <span markdown="0">\(\omega_L\)</span>. Transitions <span markdown="0">\(n\to m\)</span> then sample the matrix elements of <span markdown="0">\(a\)</span>, as in Section 2:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'_{\rm light}&=-\tfrac12\mathbf E\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf E,
\qquad a=\mathbf e_{\rm out}\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf e_{\rm in},\\
p_{\rm out}(\omega_{\rm out})
&\propto E_0\!\int\mathrm dt\,
e^{i(\omega_{\rm out}-\omega_L)t}a(t),\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta a(t)\delta a(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt
&=2\pi\sum_{n,m}p_n
|\langle m|\delta a|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right),\\
I_{\rm Raman}(\omega)&\propto
(\omega_L-\omega)^4\sum_{n,m}p_n|\langle m|\delta a|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right)\\
&=\frac{(\omega_L-\omega)^4}{2\pi}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta a(t)\delta a(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{aa}(\omega)&=
\frac{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{2\hbar}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta a(t)\delta a(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The Fourier component of the induced dipole selects transitions with energy loss <span markdown="0">\(\hbar\omega\)</span>; its squared amplitude gives the matrix-element sum above. Dipole-radiated intensity contributes <span markdown="0">\(\omega_{\rm out}^4=(\omega_L-\omega)^4\)</span>. The last line substitutes <span markdown="0">\(A=a\)</span> into quantum FDT. Optical geometry and constants are absorbed into the proportionality. [DFPT example](https://quantum-espresso.org/Doc/INPUT_DYNMAT.html).

### 3.10 Neutron or X-ray scattering

For a probe transferring momentum <span markdown="0">\(\mathbf q\)</span>, define <span markdown="0">\(\rho_{\mathbf q}=\sum_j e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r_j}\)</span>; then <span markdown="0">\(\rho_{-\mathbf q}=\rho_{\mathbf q}^\dagger\)</span>. The probe couples to <span markdown="0">\(\rho_{-\mathbf q}\)</span> and measures <span markdown="0">\(\rho_{\mathbf q}\)</span>. The inelastic dynamic structure factor is the ordered correlation divided by <span markdown="0">\(2\pi N\)</span>, where <span markdown="0">\(N\)</span> is the number of scattering particles:

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
H'&=-f_{\mathbf q}(t)\rho_{-\mathbf q},\qquad
\rho_{\mathbf q}=\sum_j e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r_j},\\
S_{\rm inel}(\mathbf q,\omega)
&=\frac{1}{2\pi N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}
\langle\delta\rho_{\mathbf q}(t)\delta\rho_{-\mathbf q}(0)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,\\
&=\frac1N\sum_{n,m}p_n
|\langle m|\delta\rho_{-\mathbf q}|n\rangle|^2
\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}\right),\\
\operatorname{Im}\chi_{\rho_{\mathbf q}\rho_{-\mathbf q}}(\omega)
&=\frac{\pi N}{\hbar}
(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})S_{\rm inel}(\mathbf q,\omega),\\
\frac{\mathrm d^2\sigma}{\mathrm d\Omega\,\mathrm d\omega}
&\propto S_{\rm inel}(\mathbf q,\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The second line follows by inserting energy eigenstates, as in Section 2. In the weak-scattering (first Born) limit, the same density matrix element gives the probe's transition probability, so its cross section is proportional to <span markdown="0">\(S_{\rm inel}\)</span>. Applying detailed balance to <span markdown="0">\(\rho_{\mathbf q},\rho_{-\mathbf q}\)</span> gives the response line from the correlation integral above. Probe-dependent factors are omitted. Add the elastic Bragg peak separately; for different atoms, put scattering lengths or form factors inside the density operator.

### 3.11 Phonon or vibrational DOS

Let <span markdown="0">\(N_q\)</span> be the number of unit cells (or sampled wave vectors), <span markdown="0">\(\nu\)</span> a phonon branch, <span markdown="0">\(Q_{\mathbf q\nu}\)</span> a mass-weighted normal coordinate, and <span markdown="0">\(a\)</span> an atom in the full sample. In a classical harmonic system, equipartition gives <span markdown="0">\(\langle\dot Q_{\mathbf q\nu}(0)^2\rangle=k_{\rm B}T\)</span>, and harmonic evolution contributes the cosine below. Summing modes and Fourier-transforming gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\dot Q_{\mathbf q\nu}(t)\dot Q_{\mathbf q\nu}(0)\rangle
&=k_{\rm B}T\cos(\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}t),\\
C_v(t)&\equiv\sum_a m_a
\langle\mathbf v_a(t)\cdot\mathbf v_a(0)\rangle
=k_{\rm B}T\sum_{\mathbf q\nu}\cos(\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}t),\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\cos(\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}t)\,\mathrm dt
&=\pi\bigl[\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})
+\delta(\omega+\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})\bigr],\\
g_{\rm ph}(\omega)
&=\frac1{N_q}\sum_{\mathbf q\nu}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})
=\frac{1}{\pi N_qk_{\rm B}T}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}C_v(t)\,\mathrm dt
\quad(\omega>0).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

The positive-frequency delta functions count modes per unit cell; they are not a current-response coefficient. The equality with an MD velocity spectrum assumes classical harmonic motion; anharmonic motion broadens the peaks. [Phonon calculation](https://www.quantum-espresso.org/Doc/INPUT_MATDYN.html); [MD spectrum](https://manual.gromacs.org/current/onlinehelp/gmx-dos.html).

### 3.12 Electronic DOS

An electronic DOS counts states at energy <span markdown="0">\(E\)</span> rather than the response of a current to a field. Use the **fermionic one-particle** retarded Green function, with electron annihilation operator <span markdown="0">\(c_i\)</span>, creation operator <span markdown="0">\(c_j^\dagger\)</span>, and anticommutator <span markdown="0">\(\{c_i,c_j^\dagger\}=c_ic_j^\dagger+c_j^\dagger c_i\)</span>. Here the energy transform is <span markdown="0">\(G^R(E)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iEt/\hbar}G^R(t)\,\mathrm dt\)</span>. For a noninteracting band of energy <span markdown="0">\(\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}\)</span>, the pole identity <span markdown="0">\(-\operatorname{Im}(x+i0^+)^{-1}/\pi=\delta(x)\)</span> gives

<div class="math-display">
$$
\begin{aligned}
G^R_{ij}(t)&=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t)
\langle\{c_i(t),c_j^\dagger(0)\}\rangle,\\
G^R_{n\mathbf k}(E)&=\frac{1}{E-\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}+i0^+},
\qquad
-\frac1\pi\operatorname{Im}G^R_{n\mathbf k}(E)
=\delta(E-\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}),\\
g_{\rm KS}(E)&=\frac1{N_k}\sum_{n\mathbf k}
\delta(E-\epsilon_{n\mathbf k}),\\
g(E)&=-\frac{1}{\pi N_k}\sum_{\mathbf k}
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Tr}_nG^R(\mathbf k,E).
\end{aligned}
$$
</div>

Here <span markdown="0">\(N_k\)</span> is the number of sampled wave vectors, <span markdown="0">\(\operatorname{Tr}_n\)</span> sums the band or orbital indices, and <span markdown="0">\(g_{\rm KS}\)</span> is the Kohn–Sham DOS obtained from DFT band energies. The last line is the interacting spectral DOS when <span markdown="0">\(G^R\)</span> includes a self-energy. The one-particle anticommutator Green function is different from the commutator response Green function in Section 2; the DOS is therefore related to spectral analysis, not another Green–Kubo coefficient. [DFT DOS example](https://quantum-espresso.org/Doc/pp_user_guide/node9.html).

## 4. MD, DFT, and the Practical Workflow

**MD:** record the observable named in the formula, subtract its equilibrium mean where required, and estimate <span markdown="0">\(C_{AB}(t)\)</span> by averaging over many starting times. Integrate it for a zero-frequency transport coefficient or Fourier-transform it for a spectrum. Check that a running transport integral reaches a stable range; check time step, trajectory length, finite-size effects, and uncertainty before interpreting a spectrum. [GROMACS' correlation-function guide](https://manual.gromacs.org/current/reference-manual/analysis/correlation-function.html) gives practical examples.

**DFT and DFPT:** ground-state DFT supplies electronic states and forces; DFPT supplies harmonic modes, effective charges, and response tensors. To use a time-correlation formula for nuclear motion, generate a trajectory with ab initio MD. To use a spectral formula, assemble transitions or mode matrix elements from the calculated states. A static DFT result alone is not a time correlation.

**Before using a formula:** write the coupling <span markdown="0">\(H'=-fB\)</span>, identify the measured <span markdown="0">\(A\)</span>, and ask whether the target is a response, a scattering spectrum, or a mode count. Then choose the time integral or finite-frequency spectrum and check whether classical or quantum statistics apply. Those choices determine the correlation and its normalization.
